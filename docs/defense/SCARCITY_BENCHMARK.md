- **species_choice**: k species drawn at random, without replacement, from the species that may enter a palette in that area and purpose (palettes.eligible_pool)
- **blocks_counts**: blocks asked B = max(k, round(tightness x U)), split as evenly as possible over the k species; trees of a species = its blocks x its trees per full block
- **points_count**: trees asked N = max(k, round(tightness x U)), at most max_trees; the palette shares them out (caps relaxed as for chosen species)
- **U**: number of squares with S >= 0.50 for at least one of the chosen species

- **Release hash**: 34964a09fe44 (v1.0-review). **Squares in the planning context**: {'rf': 8010, 'rules': 8010}.
- **Git**: branch `algorithm-fixes`, HEAD `16af3ca` plus uncommitted work (this script, its test and these outputs). **Date**: 2026-10-12.
- **Code**: production `run_plan.make_plan` for Hungarian and greedy (blocks: `species_counts`; points: `species_ids`); the random feasible baseline is the project's `matching.assign_random` on the same matrix, rebuilt in the script. Production code was not modified.
- **Plans skipped** (not creatable under the rules above): 12 rows; reasons are in the CSV (`status`).

## How to read the tables

Tightness = blocks (or trees, in points layout) asked / squares that suit at least one chosen species. A plan is *scarce* when that number is high. 'Stranded' = asked but not placed. Total W is compared only among plans where both methods place the same number of blocks; plans where they place different numbers are counted separately, because Hungarian first places as many as possible and only then maximizes W.

## What the numbers show (written by hand after the run; the tables above are generated)

**Run history (honest note).** The script was run twice with identical settings. The first run reported 694 cross-check failures: its tolerance for the 4-decimal rounding that `make_plan` applies to every planted row was too strict for plans with many rows. The placed blocks and trees were identical in every case (checked on sampled cases); only the tolerance and the way W is reported were fixed (the comparison now uses the exact rebuilt W, the production total is kept in `total_W_production`). No setting or seed changed, and I had not looked at any result table before the fix. The second run has 0 failures.

**1. Blocks layout (the main table): no scarcity occurs, so there is nothing to strand.** In 534 plans no method leaves a single tree without a spot. The achieved tightness has a median of 0.10 (90th percentile 0.49; 53 plans reach 0.5 and only 5 reach 0.9). The reason is structural: a block holds 9 to 900 trees, a plan is limited to 2,000 trees, so a plan needs few squares and the barangays have many (12 skipped cases would need more than 2,000 trees even with one block per species). Hungarian is not worse and greedy is never better, but the only visible difference is the total W: greedy gives a lower total W in 35 of 534 plans (rf; largest gap 2.70%; 17 plans and 5.50% with the rules), and never a higher one. The average W per block is almost the same (0.4799 against 0.4797).

**2. Points layout (the second table, one tree per square): scarcity appears, and Hungarian matters there.** At the tightest level (0.9) greedy places fewer blocks than Hungarian in 31 of 180 plans; at 0.6 in 4 of 180; at 0.3 in 1 of 180; in total 36 of 540 plans (7%). Hungarian never places fewer than greedy (0 of 540). Trees stranded by the matching itself: Hungarian 16, greedy 1,085, random feasible 309 (of 127,630 trees asked; the other 3,482 stranded trees are caused by the palette step and are the same for every method). The worst plan strands 87 more blocks with greedy than with Hungarian (310 trees). Among the 504 plans where both place the same number of blocks, greedy has a lower total W in 191 (largest gap 4.93%) and a higher one in none. The same picture with the rules as S source: greedy places fewer blocks in 37 of 540 plans, Hungarian in none, and the trees stranded by the matching are Hungarian 16, greedy 1,093, random feasible 308. The S source makes no meaningful difference to this comparison (nor to the blocks table).

**3. Surprise: greedy is worse than random feasible at not stranding trees** (1,085 against 309 trees stranded by the matching; at tightness 0.9: 2,491 against 1,755 stranded in all). Greedy always gives the best square to the species with the highest W, which uses up the squares that a picky species needs.

**4. The effect is smaller than the chat draft's** (21% of plans with stranded trees, 28% with lower total W, in 605 random square windows with the points layout). Here it is 7% and 38% in whole barangays. The draft chose small windows (0.5 to 1.2 km) and one-tree-per-square plans at 0.3, 0.6 and 0.9 of the suitable squares; this run uses the real barangays, the real palette (caps) and the saved S with wetness, so the two are not directly comparable. I did not test which of these differences explains the gap.

**5. Where it happens:** 29 of the 36 plans are in barangays of 500 squares or fewer (Maly 10, Malanday 6, Silangan 6, Guitnang Bayan 5), 7 in the 72 plans of larger areas; spread over all three purposes (13, 12, 11) and all species counts k = 2 to 5 (6, 11, 10, 9).

**6. Runtime is not a reason to choose greedy:** the solver alone takes a median of 0.2 to 0.3 ms for Hungarian and 0.3 to 1.2 ms for greedy; a whole `make_plan` call takes about 35 to 42 ms for either.

**What this does and does not support.** It supports: *Hungarian is needed when squares are scarce for the chosen species*, which happens in the "I have species" mode in the points layout, mostly in smaller areas and at high tightness. It does not support: that Hungarian is needed in the default blocks plans, where this benchmark finds no stranded trees and only a small total-W gain in 7% of plans. Both tables use designed tightness levels (0.3, 0.6, 0.9) and random species sets; how often real planners meet such tight cases is unknown.

## Which part of the app produces the points layout, and can a normal user reach it?

**Short answer: no. A normal dashboard user can only get the blocks layout.** Checked in the code on 12 Oct 2026:

- **The dashboard never asks for the points layout.** The plan request it builds (`frontend/src/new/AppNew.jsx`, `planBody`) contains only the purpose, the area, and either `n_saplings` (goal "I have an area") or `species_counts` (goal "I have species"). It has no `layout_mode` field, and no screen, button, setting or help text offers a choice of layout. The words "points layout" or "one tree per square" appear nowhere in the dashboard's screens.
- **The service then uses its default, which is blocks:** `API_CFG["layout_mode"]` (`api_v2.py`) is taken from `run_plan.CFG["layout_mode"] = "blocks"`. So the same two goals always give block plans, and the progress, "Planted" status and top-up features exist only for block plans.
- **Ways to get points plans (none of them on a screen):**
  1. the API: send `"layout_mode": "points"` in `POST /plan-event` (it is documented in the interactive API page at `http://localhost:8001/docs`; with `species_counts` too, a case that `tests/test_species_counts.py` covers);
  2. the command line: `python pipeline/run_plan.py --layout-mode points`;
  3. a code or configuration change of the default (`API_CFG["layout_mode"]` or `run_plan.CFG["layout_mode"]`);
  4. plans saved before round 10a (5 Oct 2026) load as points plans; three 300-tree plans of that date are in `data/processed/plans/`.
- The old dashboard (`#/legacy`) does not use these algorithms at all (its scores are computed in JavaScript).

**What this means for the wording of the thesis.**
- The result that matters for users is the **blocks table**: in the plans a user can actually make, no method strands a tree (0 of 534 plans) and Hungarian gives only a small total-W gain in 35 of 534 plans (largest 2.70%). Do not write that Hungarian "prevents stranded trees in the app" on the basis of this benchmark.
- The **points table** is a **stress test of the matching algorithm** on the earlier one-tree-per-square formulation, which the code still contains and which the API and the command line can still run. It shows what the Hungarian algorithm does when squares are scarce (greedy strands 1,085 trees against 16, in 36 of 540 plans, 31 of them at the highest tightness). It supports the claim "Hungarian is the right solver when a plan competes for scarce squares", not the claim "users meet this situation in the dashboard".
- Honest framing: *"In the block layout used by the dashboard, squares are rarely scarce and greedy and Hungarian place the same trees; the difference appears when the layout assigns one tree per square (API and command line), where Hungarian strands almost no trees and greedy strands up to 87 blocks more in a plan."* Whether a block-based plan could become scarce in practice (for example for a species with very few suitable squares in a small barangay at the 2,000-tree limit) was not found in these 534 plans; the 2,000-tree limit and the block capacity keep the number of squares needed small.
